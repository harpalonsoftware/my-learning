Two-Layer Connection Pooling: HikariCP + PgBouncer

“If PgBouncer is a connection pooler, do I still need an app-side pool like HikariCP?” Yes — usually both. They pool different connections, on different sides of the wire, for different reasons.

← Extends Part 3 §6 (PgBouncer) — read that first for the connection-storm setup

01

The big picture — two pools, two scopes

Both are called “connection pools,” which is exactly why it’s confusing. The difference is where they live and what they pool:

   ┌──────────── one app server (one JVM) ───────────┐
   │   threads ──► HikariCP ───┐                       │
   │  (your code)  (pool of app↔proxy connections)    │
   └──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────┘
                              │   ...and 49 OTHER app servers do the same
                              ▼
                         [ PgBouncer ] ───► Postgres
                         (pool of proxy↔DB connections, shared by ALL apps)

HikariCP pools connections inside one app process — between your code’s threads and whatever it dials. It lives in your JVM. PgBouncer pools connections between many app processes and the database — a separate server in front of Postgres. (Sources: HikariCP — github.com/brettwooldridge/HikariCP; PgBouncer — pgbouncer.org. Confident.)

The dividing idea

They aren’t duplicates stacked pointlessly — they pool at two different scopes. HikariCP is per-process (local). PgBouncer is cluster-wide (global). One optimizes your app’s local access to connections; the other protects the database from the sum of all apps.

03

Why you keep the app-side pool anyway

The reason people think PgBouncer makes HikariCP redundant: “they’re both pools.” But the app-side pool does three jobs PgBouncer cannot, because it lives where your code runs:

1 · It makes “get a connection” free on the hot path

Without an app-side pool, every query does a fresh TCP connect + (if TLS) handshake + auth — even just to reach PgBouncer. With HikariCP, a thread grabs an already-open connection from a local pool in microseconds. Making connection-acquisition essentially free from the app’s point of view is HikariCP’s entire reason to exist.

2 · It bounds and queues your app’s own concurrency

Pool size is a backpressure valve: set max = 20 and your app will never run more than 20 queries at once — the 21st thread waits locally. That protects your app from spawning unbounded DB work and shields everything downstream. PgBouncer can’t apply this limit per app instance.

3 · It’s the integration point with your framework

Your ORM/driver (Spring, Hibernate, etc.) expects a DataSource that hands out connections — that’s HikariCP’s slot. PgBouncer is invisible to your code; it just looks like “the database.”

Name the trap · “both are pools, so one is redundant”

They pool at different layers for different reasons. HikariCP optimizes the app’s local access (latency, backpressure, framework integration). PgBouncer optimizes the database’s global connection count. Drop HikariCP and every query pays a fresh connect cost to reach PgBouncer — you’d hammer it with connect/disconnect churn. Drop PgBouncer and the DB sees the sum of every app’s pool. They protect different victims.

04

The analogy, and the numbers that prove it

Analogy

The database is the kitchen, with a hard cap on chefs. HikariCP is one restaurant branch’s own roster of waiters — Branch A keeps 20 on staff so its guests never wait for someone to be hired and trained mid-service (local efficiency). PgBouncer is the shared head-office scheduler all branches route through, keeping the total chefs the kitchen must staff sane — because if A wants 20, B wants 20, C wants 20… the kitchen would need 60 chefs it can’t afford.

The analogy makes the key fact obvious: with many app instances, the app-side pools add up.

   50 instances  x  HikariCP max 20  =  1000 connections demanded of Postgres
                                          (the connection storm from Part 3 again)

   HikariCP alone CAN'T fix this — no single instance knows about the others.
   PgBouncer collapses that 1000 down to ~20 real backends.
The clean split

HikariCP solves: “this one app instance accesses connections fast and bounded.” PgBouncer solves: “the sum of all instances doesn’t melt the database.” Different scopes — so you typically want both.

Retrieve it

In one sentence each: what victim does HikariCP protect, and what victim does PgBouncer protect? (One is “this app instance,” one is “the database as a whole.”)

05

What each connection actually is — watch the flow

The hidden assumption that makes this confusing: you picture one pipe running from a thread all the way to the database. That pipe does not exist. There’s a chain of separate, independently-counted links, and a pooler’s whole job is to break the 1-to-1 between them.

Every link below that is a socket is a real TCP socket — a real OS file descriptor (the same FDs from Part 1). Nothing is virtual. “Pooled” doesn’t mean fake; it means kept open and reused. A socket is only ever in one of two states: in use (a query is flowing through it) or idle but open (kept warm, costing almost nothing).

Reading the three boundaries

Boundary 1 — threads borrow from the pool (no socket). A thread doesn’t own a connection; it leases one of the 4 pool sockets for the length of a query, then returns it. That checkout happens inside the JVM — no network, no socket. So “the app has many connections” is loose talk: it has many threads competing for a capped set of sockets.

Boundary 2 — HikariCP ↔ PgBouncer (real sockets, one socket / two names). Each of the 4 lines is one real TCP socket. From HikariCP’s end it’s “a pooled connection”; from PgBouncer’s end the same socket is “a client connection.” One wire, two names. Per instance that’s 20 in the real scenario; ×10 instances = ~200 arriving at PgBouncer.

Boundary 3 — PgBouncer ↔ Postgres (fewer real sockets, time-shared). Different, separate sockets — and far fewer. A query holds one only while its transaction runs; on commit it’s released for the next query from any client. Watch a server socket go blue for one query, free, then green for another — that reuse is why ~200 client sockets need only ~25 server sockets: at any instant only ~25 queries are actually executing.

Name the trap

There is no single end-to-end pipe. PgBouncer holds two independent sets of real sockets — ~200 on its client side, ~25 on its server side — because it deliberately refuses to keep them 1-to-1. “The app has more, the pooler has fewer” just means: more client-side sockets than server-side sockets, on the same box. Everything is a real socket; the only magic is reuse (pooling) and time-sharing (the scarce server side).

The bridge model — who opens the “real” connection

A common follow-on confusion: “so the app’s connections aren’t real DB connections, only PgBouncer’s are?” Not quite. A database connection is a TCP socket + an authenticated database-protocol session. The trick is that PgBouncer speaks the PostgreSQL wire protocol on both sides — it impersonates Postgres to your app (does the login handshake, accepts SQL) and acts as a client to the real Postgres on the other side. So the app↔PgBouncer link is a genuine, authenticated DB session; it simply terminates at PgBouncer instead of at Postgres. (Source: pgbouncer.org. Confident.)

This is the TLS-bridging pattern again

Exactly like an L7 proxy that terminates TLS and re-originates a new encrypted session to the backend (Part 2), PgBouncer terminates your DB session and re-originates its own session to a real backend it manages. So your instinct “PgBouncer opens the real DB connections” is correct if “real” means the backend connection to actual Postgres. The fix to the mental model: the app’s connection isn’t a bare socket — it’s a real DB session to the bridge. (ProxySQL does the identical thing in the MySQL protocol.)

One consequence you may have hit: in transaction pooling mode, when HikariCP opens its connections at startup, PgBouncer authenticates those client sessions but does not necessarily open a backend per client — it grabs a backend only when a transaction actually runs, releasing it on commit. So you can have many warm client sessions and only a few backend sessions behind them. That gap is the decoupling, made literal. (Source: pgbouncer.org, pooling modes. Confident.)

Why the app “complains” at startup if the DB is unreachable

This is the proof these are real sessions, not bare sockets. HikariCP defaults to fail-fast: initializationFailTimeout defaults to 1 ms, meaning at construction it tries to acquire and validate one initial connection, and if it can’t, it throws and the pool/app fails to start. A bare TCP socket wouldn’t do this — the complaint happens precisely because HikariCP must establish a valid, authenticated DB session (it validates via JDBC4 isValid() by default). (Source: HikariCP README + DeepWiki config docs. Confident; exact eager-prefill-to-minimumIdle behavior varies by version — verify.)

Retrieve it

At a random instant your 200 client sockets are open but PgBouncer keeps 25 server sockets. Roughly how many of the 200 are tied to a server socket right now — and what are the other ~175 doing that makes it safe for them to hold no server socket?

06

Inside PgBouncer: the limits, and the waiting queue

Two questions fall out of the bridge model: when more queries arrive than there are backend connections, what happens? And who actually sets “the number of connections”?

Extra queries wait — but be precise about what waits

When every backend connection in a pool is busy, an incoming query queues until one frees up. The precise part: the client connection stays fully connected to PgBouncer the whole time — it’s the query/transaction that’s parked, not the connection that’s dropped. The wait is bounded by query_wait_timeout (default 120 s); exceed it and the query is cancelled with an error. An optional reserve pool (reserve_pool_size) opens a few extra backends for bursts after a query has waited reserve_pool_timeout seconds. You can watch it live: SHOW POOLS exposes cl_waiting (queued clients) and maxwait (oldest waiter). (Source: pgbouncer.org config; OneUptime PgBouncer guide. Confident.)

Who sets the numbers — you do, in pgbouncer.ini

There are two different numbers, mapping onto the two sides of the bridge:

ParameterGovernsDefaultSide
max_client_conntotal client connections accepted100the “many” side
default_pool_sizereal backend connections per pool (one pool = one user+database pair)20the “few” side
min_pool_sizebackends kept warm when idle0few
reserve_pool_sizeextra backends for bursts0few
max_db_connectionshard cap on backends to one database0 (unlimited)few
max_user_connectionscap on backends for one user0 (unlimited)few

So “the number of real DB connections open at a time” is set by default_pool_size, fenced by max_db_connections, with reserve_pool_size as a burst valve. The number of clients allowed is the separate max_client_conn. (Source: pgbouncer.org config; default_pool_size=20 corroborated by Crunchy/DZone. Confident on the named params and on 20 / 100 / 120 s; confirm reserve/min defaults with SHOW CONFIG on your build.)

Name the trap · default_pool_size is per pool, not global

A “pool” is one (user, database) pair. Ten databases each getting 20 backends is up to 200 — which can blow past Postgres’s max_connections. That’s what max_db_connections and max_user_connections fence in. The true ceiling above everything is Postgres max_connections; PgBouncer’s job is to keep the sum of all its pools under it.

How to pick default_pool_size? It’s the Little’s-Law point from earlier: roughly peak concurrent transactions × average transaction time. One worked example: 500 req/s × 20 ms ≈ 10 connections, plus headroom to ~15–20. (Source: OneUptime — a rule of thumb; measure your real avg_query_time via SHOW STATS rather than trusting the formula blindly.)

07

PgBouncer vs ProxySQL: both pool, one also routes

The misconception to kill: “PgBouncer limits connections, ProxySQL splits read/write, so the connection count stays the same on ProxySQL.” No — ProxySQL pools and multiplexes too. Read/write splitting is something it does on top of pooling, not instead of it.

ProxySQL’s connection multiplexing is a core feature, on by default: many frontend connections share a small set of backend connections. The docs put it plainly — many client connections (tens of thousands) can use a few backend connections (a few hundred); a backend is returned to the pool when a request completes, not when the client disconnects, provided it can be safely reused. MySQL is “thread per connection” like Postgres, so collapsing connections matters just as much. (Source: proxysql.com — Multiplexing, MySQL Connection Pooling, FAQ. Confident.)

PgBouncerProxySQL
Connection pooling / multiplexingyesyes (also collapses many→few)
Read/write split, query routingnoyes
Query caching, rewriting, firewallnoyes
Database familyPostgreSQLMySQL / MariaDB family

So ProxySQL is a superset on the routing side: it does everything a pooler does, plus query-aware routing. PgBouncer deliberately stays minimal. And they live in different ecosystems — PgBouncer is Postgres-only, ProxySQL is MySQL-family. You pick the one matching your DB; you don’t normally chain them. (The “three tiers” in Part 3 were a ladder of how much intelligence a proxy has, not a literal stack. For Postgres read/write routing you’d reach for Pgpool-II or PgCat, not PgBouncer + ProxySQL.)

When multiplexing switches off (and why it’s the same rule as PgBouncer)

A backend can only be shared while it’s stateless. ProxySQL pins a backend to one frontend whenever the session carries state that mustn’t leak: an active transaction (pinned until commit/rollback), autocommit=0, user-defined @variables, LOCK TABLES/GET_LOCK(), temp tables, and prepared statements. In fact, once a client prepares a statement via the binary protocol, ProxySQL gives it a dedicated backend — and long-lived prepared statements can pin connections, shrinking the effective pool so exhaustion hits before the configured max. That is the same underlying rule as PgBouncer’s transaction-mode gotchas: a stateful connection can’t be time-shared. (Source: proxysql.com — Multiplexing, Prepared Statements. Confident.)

When you might genuinely drop one

“Always both” is lazy. The honest edges:

SituationApp pool (HikariCP)External pool (PgBouncer)
Single app instance, low concurrencyKeep itOften unneeded — the pools don’t sum to anything dangerous
Many app instances hitting PostgresKeep itAdd it — collapses the summed connections
Serverless / massively horizontal (Lambda)Nearly useless (ephemeral, can’t reuse)Essential — instance count is huge & spiky
Managed pooler already (RDS Proxy, Supabase)Keep itThat slot is filled — don’t add a second
The serverless pattern (external pooler essential, app pool near-trivial) is well established in shape; verify the exact recommendation for your platform/runtime, as the specifics differ by provider.

09

The configuration trap nobody warns you about

Run both with PgBouncer in transaction pooling mode (the recommended default from Part 3) and there’s a real interaction: HikariCP holds a connection open assuming it’s a stable session, while PgBouncer reassigns the underlying backend per transaction. So anything session-scoped — a SET, a temp table, a server-side prepared statement — was created on one backend but your next transaction may land on a different one that’s never seen it.

Name the trap — and the version that changed it

Prepared statements were the classic casualty — but this is exactly the kind of claim that goes stale, so here’s the version-accurate picture. Before PgBouncer 1.21 (Oct 2023), prepared statements only worked in session pooling mode; in transaction mode the only fix was to disable client-side prepared statements. Since PgBouncer 1.21, you can set max_prepared_statements to a non-zero value and PgBouncer tracks them, re-preparing on the linked backend on the fly. So today the options are: (a) upgrade to 1.21+ and set max_prepared_statements, or (b) disable client-side prepared statements — for JDBC, by adding prepareThreshold=0 to the connection string. SET commands and temp tables that must persist across transactions still need a direct/session connection.

Sources: PgBouncer FAQ & the 1.21.0 / 1.22.0 release notes (pgbouncer.org); Crunchy Data & pganalyze write-ups on the 1.21 feature; PlanetScale PgBouncer docs. Confidence: high on the before/after-1.21 behavior, the max_prepared_statements mechanism, and the JDBC prepareThreshold=0 workaround. Real-world caveat (verify for your churn/pool size): even on 1.21+, the first prepare on a fresh backend is a real round trip, so very high statement churn with a small pool can add latency. Confirm exact behavior for your driver and PgBouncer version before relying.

10

FAQ & misconceptions — every doubt, settled

The exact questions from this whole thread, distilled with the source that backs each answer.

Q1 · PgBouncer is a pool and HikariCP is a pool — isn’t one redundant?

No. They pool at different scopes. HikariCP is per app process (fast local access, per-instance backpressure, framework integration); PgBouncer is cluster-wide (collapses the summed connections so the DB survives). Drop HikariCP and every query pays a fresh connect to the pooler; drop PgBouncer and the DB sees the sum of all app pools.

Source: HikariCP README; pgbouncer.org. (§1, §3, §4)

Q2 · Are the app’s connections “just sockets,” with only PgBouncer holding real DB connections?

No. A DB connection = TCP socket + authenticated protocol session. The app↔PgBouncer link is a real Postgres session; it just terminates at PgBouncer, which speaks the Postgres protocol on both sides and re-originates its own backend session. “PgBouncer opens the real (backend) connections” is correct; “the app side isn’t a DB connection” is the error.

Source: pgbouncer.org. (§5)

Q3 · Why does the app complain at startup if the DB is unreachable?

HikariCP is fail-fast: initializationFailTimeout defaults to 1 ms, so it tries to acquire and validate one connection at construction and throws if it can’t. A bare socket wouldn’t do that — the complaint proves these are real, validated DB sessions.

Source: HikariCP README + DeepWiki config. (§5)

Q4 · When more queries arrive than backends, does PgBouncer hold them waiting?

Yes — the query queues (the client stays connected), bounded by query_wait_timeout (default 120 s); reserve_pool_size adds burst capacity. Watch cl_waiting / maxwait in SHOW POOLS.

Source: pgbouncer.org config; OneUptime. (§6)

Q5 · Who defines the number of connections PgBouncer opens?

You do, in pgbouncer.ini. Backend count = default_pool_size (default 20, per (user, database) pool), fenced by max_db_connections; client count = max_client_conn (default 100). The hard ceiling above all pools and instances is Postgres’s own max_connections.

Source: pgbouncer.org config; default 20 corroborated by Crunchy/DZone. (§6)

Q6 · ProxySQL only splits read/write — so the connection count stays the same, right?

No. ProxySQL multiplexes/pools too (on by default): tens of thousands of client connections can share a few hundred backend connections. Read/write splitting is layered on top of pooling. ProxySQL is a superset; PgBouncer pools only.

Source: proxysql.com (Multiplexing, FAQ). (§7)

Q7 · Do I run PgBouncer and ProxySQL together?

Normally no — different ecosystems. PgBouncer is Postgres; ProxySQL is MySQL-family. Pick the one for your DB. For Postgres read/write routing, use Pgpool-II or PgCat, not PgBouncer.

Source: pgbouncer.org; proxysql.com. (§7)

Q8 · Does transaction mode still break prepared statements?

Only before PgBouncer 1.21 (Oct 2023). Since 1.21, set max_prepared_statements > 0 and they work in transaction mode; for JDBC the disable path is prepareThreshold=0. Even on 1.21+, the first prepare on a fresh backend is a real round trip.

Source: pgbouncer.org FAQ + 1.21/1.22 notes; Crunchy Data; pganalyze. (§9)

Q9 · What turns multiplexing off (in either tool)?

Session state that can’t be shared: an active transaction, SET/session vars, temp tables, advisory locks, and prepared statements. While “dirty,” the backend is pinned to one client and can’t be time-shared — the single rule behind every pooling gotcha here.

Source: proxysql.com (Multiplexing); pgbouncer.org (pooling modes). (§7, §9)

Chew on this later

Diffuse-mode seed

This is the same recursive pattern from Part 3’s “who balances the balancer” seed in new clothes — pooling appears at every layer where a scarce resource is shared (threads→connections inside the app; app-connections→DB-backends at the proxy). So stack the numbers: HikariCP max 20 per instance, 50 instances, PgBouncer pool of 25, Postgres max_connections of 100 — and a true thundering herd hits. Where exactly does the queue form, and which ceiling bites first? Trace one request’s wait through all three limits. Getting that trace right is the difference between “I configured some pools” and “I understand where my system actually blocks under load.”
