RAG, without the hand-waving

Retrieval-Augmented Generation, ground up: why it exists, what an embedding model really is, how to choose one, and whether you need a GPU. One analogy, all the way down — every claim sourced.

01 · the big picture

Two letters doing all the work

RAG = Retrieval-Augmented Generation. In one breath: before the model answers, you fetch the most relevant documents from a knowledge source and paste them into the prompt — so it answers from those facts, not from whatever it half-remembers. It bolts a search step in front of a language model. Tap to split it.

GENERATEanswer using them

The term and original architecture — a neural retriever feeding a sequence-to-sequence generator — come from Lewis et al., Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, NeurIPS 2020. (arXiv:2005.11401)
02 · why this exists

Feel the gap first

A plain language model is a sharp student who crammed, then sat a closed-book exam. Three gaps open up:

1 · Knowledge cutoff. It only "studied" up to a training date — anything newer, it can't know.

2 · No access to your private/changing data. It never read your wiki, your internal docs, your codebase.

3 · It can fill gaps with plausible-but-unsupported answers. When it lacks the facts, a model can still produce fluent, confident text that isn't grounded in any source — a well-documented failure mode of generative models. (Ji et al., Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation, ACM Computing Surveys, 2023 — arXiv:2202.03629)

But RAG isn't the only fix. The honest framing: the "augment" half is just "the model answers using context you supplied." The whole game is who decides what's relevant — a human up front, or a machine at query time.

ApproachWhat it doesWins atLoses at
Just ask the LLMAnswer from trained weights (closed book)cheap, fast, general knowledgecutoff, no private data, ungrounded gaps
Fine-tuningRetrain the model on your data, into the weightsteaching behaviour / style / formatcostly, slow to update, hard to cite a source
Stuff it all in contextPaste every doc into the prompt every timetiny doc sets, one-off taskscost per token, finite window, mid-context drop-off
RAGFetch only the relevant bits at query timelarge / changing / private facts, citable answersquality capped by retrieval; more moving parts

Fine-tuning changes how the model behaves. RAG changes what facts are on the desk.

on the "stuff it all in" failure
Even with a large context window, models can use information in the middle of a long input less reliably than at the ends — the "lost in the middle" effect — so "just paste everything" degrades as the corpus grows. (Liu et al., Lost in the Middle, 2023 — arXiv:2307.03172)
↻ quick recall
Your wiki gains a new policy page daily and you want answers from the latest ones. Fine-tuning or RAG — why?
RAG. The knowledge changes daily; retraining per change is absurdly slow and costly. With RAG you just update the documents and the next query picks them up — and you can cite which page. Fine-tuning is for changing behaviour, not chasing fast-moving facts.
When NOT to use RAG: the corpus is small enough to just paste; the facts are stable and already in the model; or you need a new skill/style, not fact recall. RAG is the machinery for deciding what to add when you can't do it by hand. No hand-deciding needed → no RAG needed.
03 · the anchor

One analogy, all the way down

Picture a sharp student taking an exam. No RAG = closed-book → answers from memory. RAG = open-book: an assistant sprints to the library, grabs the few relevant pages, drops them on the desk; the student reads those, then writes.

Everything else — the model, embeddings, BM25, cosine, dimensions, GPUs —
is just "how does the assistant find the right pages fast, in a library of millions?"

We never switch metaphors. Hold onto the desk.

04 · embeddings

Turning meaning into numbers

Analogy beat. A keyword index files pages by exact word. But "I forgot my login" and "reset your password" share zero words — a word-matching librarian misses the perfect page. So we build an index that files pages by what they're about.

     a "meaning map" — really hundreds of dimensions, drawn as 2-D

              cat ●
              ● kitten
       ● puppy
     dog ●                       "reset password" ●
                                  "forgot my login" ●  ← land near
                                                          each other
       ● banana                   ● account recovery
      fruit ●
              ── close = similar meaning · far = different ──

Plain English. An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) standing for the meaning of some text. Similar meaning → numerically close vectors. To search, embed the question, drop it on the map, grab the nearest pages.

literally true
The idea that geometry in vector space encodes meaning — and is learned from data, not hand-set — traces to word vectors like word2vec, where directions captured relationships (famously, king − man + woman ≈ queen). (Mikolov et al., Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space, 2013 — arXiv:1301.3781.)
↻ predict
If embeddings place "earth is flat" and "earth is round" close together (same topic), what does retrieval actually find?
Text that's on-topic, not text that's correct. Embeddings encode similarity of subject, with no notion of truth. Two contradictory claims about the same thing sit close. Keep this in your pocket.
05 · what the model is

The grinder, not the grounds

The keystone question: if the output is "just an N-number array," why can't any program make it — why a whole model? Here it is in one line:

Producing N numbers is trivial.
Producing N numbers where distance = closeness in meaning is the hard part — and that mapping can't be hand-written, it must be learned.

An embedding model is a frozen function: text in → vector out. Inside sit millions of learned weights — tuned once during training, then fixed. Those weights are the model. It does not store your documents; it holds the trained recipe for placing any text into meaning-space.

forgot my login
embedding modelfrozen weights · the "judgment"
0.11 -0.02 0.85…

the grinder (machine) keeps running · the grounds (vectors) appear only when you feed it text

Why a simple program can't. Sit down to write text_to_vector() by hand and you hit a wall fast:

  Attempt 1 — HASH the text:
     "password"  → [random numbers]
     "passwordd" → [totally different]   one typo ⇒ far away
     "login"     → [unrelated]          synonym ⇒ far away
  ✗ geometry carries ZERO meaning.

  Attempt 2 — COUNT words (bag of words):
     "reset password" → reset=1, password=1
     "forgot login"   → forgot=1, login=1
  ✗ no shared words ⇒ vectors point apart  (this IS the BM25 problem)

Meaning is bottomless — "bank" (river vs money), "not good" (negation flips it). No human writes rules for all of it. So instead of writing the mapping, we learn it: show a neural network billions of examples with a contrastive goal — pairs that should match come out close, unrelated pairs come out far — and nudge the weights until it generalises. Tap to watch random numbers become a learned map:

animals account / login food
literally true
Modern text embedders are neural networks (typically transformer encoders) trained so similar text maps to nearby vectors. Anthropic's own docs describe embedding models as neural networks that capture and compress semantic context. The "similar-close / different-far" contrastive recipe is standard. (Reimers & Gurevych, Sentence-BERT, EMNLP 2019 — arXiv:1908.10084; Karpukhin et al., Dense Passage Retrieval, EMNLP 2020 — arXiv:2004.04906; transformer architecture: Vaswani et al., 2017 — arXiv:1706.03762; Anthropic Embeddings docs, docs.claude.com.)
So what does "N dimensions" hold? N is just how many coordinates the model outputs to pin a position. Each number is an abstract learned "feature axis" — and usually it has no human name; the model discovered these directions itself in training. The vector doesn't hold the text or its words — it holds where this text sits relative to all other text.
↻ recall
A Python one-liner turns any text into 768 random-but-consistent numbers (same text always → same array). Is that an embedding model? Why / why not?
No. It's consistent but meaningless: similar texts land nowhere near each other. An embedding model is valuable because its learned weights make "close in numbers" mean "close in meaning." The arithmetic of emitting numbers is trivial; knowing which numbers to emit is the learned, can't-fake-it part.
06 · keyword search (BM25)

The old librarian, and why you keep them

Analogy beat. Before the meaning-map librarian came the word-counting one: BM25. It ranks a page by how well its words match the query's, with three instincts:

  BM25 score = sum over each query word of:
    (1) term frequency   — appears a lot here? (saturating: 50 hits ≠ 50×)
    (2) inverse doc freq  — a RARE, informative word? ("borewell" ≫ "the")
    (3) length norm      — don't let a giant doc win by sheer size

Why it can't do the meaning job: it's a bag of words — "login credentials" and "password" are just different strings. But the twist: BM25 nails the exact, rare tokens embeddings tend to blur — a part number RD-1250, error code ENOENT, a surname, a slug. Serious systems often run both and merge ("hybrid search").

Not BM25 vs embeddings. BM25 = exact words · embeddings = meaning · you often want both.

literally true
BM25 ("Best Matching 25", Okapi family) comes from the probabilistic-relevance work of Robertson, Spärck Jones and colleagues; term-frequency saturation and length normalization are tuned by parameters k₁ and b. (Robertson & Zaragoza, The Probabilistic Relevance Framework: BM25 and Beyond, Foundations and Trends in IR, 2009.)
↻ recall
A user searches the exact error code ENOENT. Trust embeddings or BM25 here — and why?
BM25. Exact, rare token, no paraphrase to resolve — just a precise string. Embeddings might blur it toward generic "file error" text and miss the exact hit. This is where the old librarian beats the meaning map — and why hybrid search exists.
07 · cosine similarity

Measure the angle, ignore the length

Analogy beat. Two vectors point somewhere on the map. Cosine similarity asks one thing — do these arrows point the same way? — and ignores how long they are. Drag the query and watch the ranking reshuffle.

drag the query arrow (now at 28°)

Plain English. Cosine = dot product divided by both lengths, scoring pure direction: +1 same way, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite. Direction carries meaning; length often just reflects passage length.

Notice: "banana bread" has the longest arrow yet ranks last whenever the query points away. Length can't rescue a bad direction — that's the point of cosine.
calibrated
Cosine is the common default, not the only metric — dot product and Euclidean (L2) are also used. If vectors are normalized to length 1 (many models output them so), cosine, dot product and L2 rank in the same order. Otherwise use the metric the model's card specifies. (standard linear-algebra / information-retrieval practice; provider model cards.)
↻ predict
You double a document vector's length but keep its direction. What happens to its cosine with a query?
Nothing. Cosine divides out both lengths, so only the angle matters. The dot product would change, since it doesn't normalize.
08 · dimensions (N)

Who decides how many numbers?

Analogy beat. Think of N as how many slots the librarian writes on each index card. A 384-slot card captures less nuance than a 3072-slot one — but costs more to store and search. The library fixed that format when it trained the librarian. You don't pick N; you pick a model, and its N is frozen — the same N for every text it embeds.

literally true · verified dimensions
N is fixed at training time. Real examples: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 → 384, all-mpnet-base-v2 → 768, Cohere embed-v3 → 1024, OpenAI text-embedding-3-small → 1536, -large → 3072, Voyage voyage-3-large → 1024. Higher N can capture richer meaning but with diminishing returns and more cost — so people benchmark on their own data, not a leaderboard. (sentence-transformers / OpenAI / Cohere / Voyage model cards; MTEB benchmark: Muennighoff et al., 2022 — arXiv:2210.07316.)
The one twist that gives you a say — Matryoshka. Some models pack the most important meaning into the front of the vector, so you can chop the tail (keep the first 256 of 1536) with a small accuracy hit — like nested dolls. (Matryoshka Representation Learning, Kusupati et al., NeurIPS 2022 — arXiv:2205.13147; supported by e.g. OpenAI text-embedding-3, Cohere v4, Nomic v1.5 — check the model card.)
↻ recall
Do you ask "how many dimensions does this particular document need"?
No. N is a property of the model, never the text. Every document and query from one model comes out at the same N. Uniform length is the point — you can only compare vectors of equal length in the same space.
09 · embedder ≠ generator

"I'm on Opus 4.8 — which Opus embedding?"

This is the question almost everyone hits, and it's a category error. The chat/generation model and the embedding model are two different components you choose independently. There is no "Opus embedding."

   RAG has TWO model slots — pick each separately:

   ┌─────────────────────────┐        ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │  EMBEDDER (the "R")       │        │  GENERATOR (the "G")      │
   │  text → vector          │        │  prompt → answer         │
   │                         │   +    │                          │
   │  voyage-3, openai       │        │  Opus, GPT, local Llama  │
   │  embeddings, nomic      │        │                          │
   │  (Ollama), all-MiniLM   │        │  ← a chat model lives    │
   │                         │        │     HERE only            │
   └─────────────────────────┘        └──────────────────────────┘
        builds the meaning-map             never touches embeddings

You can run Opus 4.8 as the generator and a local Ollama embedder at once — different vendors, no problem. They don't talk directly: the embedder fills the vector store; the generator reads whatever chunks the search returns. ("ChatGPT" is likewise a chat product — its embeddings come from a separate OpenAI endpoint, not the chat model.)

literally true
Anthropic does not offer its own embedding model; its docs point to third-party providers (Voyage AI is named as preferred) while stating any quality embedding model works with Claude. Using a separate embedding API alongside the generator is standard practice. (Anthropic Embeddings documentation, docs.claude.com / platform.claude.com; anthropics/claude-cookbooks VoyageAI guide.)
↻ recall
You picked Opus 4.8 to generate answers. Which "Opus embedding model" do you use to build the index?
Trick question — there isn't one. Opus is a generator. You choose a separate embedder (Voyage, OpenAI's embeddings endpoint, or a local model). The two slots are independent; only the generator is "Opus."
10 · choosing an embedder

What forces the choice (almost nothing) — and what should

Nothing external forces a particular embedder. Two hard constraints box you in: (1) consistency — the model that embeds your documents must embed your queries (switching later = re-embed everything); (2) your vector store must match the model's dimension N and recommended metric. Beyond that, it's your call — and a bad call just means quietly worse retrieval, which (per the trap) caps the whole system.

The factors that should decide it:

FactorWhy it mattersConcrete gotcha
Hosted vs localprivacy + cost + who runs the GPUSensitive data? A local model keeps it on your machine.
Max input tokenscaps the chunk size you can embedall-MiniLM-L6-v2 ≈ 256 tokens — bigger chunks get truncated; voyage-3-large ≈ 32k.
Retrieval qualitythe whole point; the ceiling on RAGLeaderboards (MTEB) filter, don't decide — test on your data; rankings flip by domain.
Dimension Nstorage + search speed3072-dim is ~8× the footprint of 384-dim. Bigger ≠ better.
Domain / languagegeneric models miss jargon & non-EnglishVendors ship domain models (code, finance, legal).
Cost per token (hosted)adds up over millions of docsCheapest-per-token isn't cheapest if it re-indexes more.
The guideline doc you were looking for is the model card. Every embedding model publishes one stating its fixed dimension, max input tokens, recommended distance metric, whether outputs are normalized, and often a query vs document distinction. Voyage, for instance, lets you set an input_type for queries vs documents to improve retrieval, and notes embeddings made with and without it stay compatible. (model cards; anthropics/claude-cookbooks VoyageAI guide.)

"So why ever pay? Local is free."

Mostly a fair instinct — local is the right default for private data, small-to-medium English corpora, and prototypes. But "free" only means no API invoice; the bill moves elsewhere:

   PAID API embedder             LOCAL embedder
   $ per token                 $0 per token
   data leaves machine         data stays private        ✓
   zero infra to run           YOU run + scale the hardware
   vendor handles updates      YOU manage versions / uptime
   often higher quality        quality varies by model
        pay in money                  pay in time + hardware + ops
The decision rule: start local and free; measure retrieval on your own queries; reach for paid only when a specific factor forces it — weak retrieval on your data, inputs longer than the local model accepts, a specialised domain, multilingual needs, or scale where running it yourself costs more than the API.
↻ recall
You ship a 384-dim local embedder, then six months later want a 1024-dim model for better retrieval. What's the real migration cost?
You must re-embed the entire corpus and rebuild the index — old and new vectors live in different spaces and different lengths, so they can't be compared. That's the "never mix models" + "fixed N" traps combined: changing the embedder is a full re-index, never a patch.
11 · do you need a GPU?

It's a throughput question, not a requirement

Why a GPU helps: turning text into a vector means multiplying it through millions of weights — matrix multiplication, where the steps are independent. A CPU has a few fast cores built for sequential, latency-sensitive work; a GPU has thousands of small cores built for doing the same multiply on lots of data at once. Press run and race them on "embed 12 chunks":

CPU — a few cores, one-ish at a time

GPU — thousands of cores, big batches at once

literally true · calibrated
CPUs are latency-oriented (few powerful cores, large caches, branch prediction); GPUs are throughput-oriented (thousands of small SIMD cores) and so excel at the matrix math at the heart of neural networks. Crucially, the GPU's edge only appears at scale — for small networks/workloads a CPU can match or beat a GPU. (latency-vs-throughput design & GPU matrix-multiply advantage: arXiv:2509.04594; "for smaller networks the CPU is faster than the GPU": HSF Machine Learning on GPU training material.)

Two things soften this a lot:

1 · You embed documents once, offline. The bulk pass is a background job, not something a user waits on — overnight on a CPU may be fine for a one-time build; you only re-run it for new docs. 2 · Query-time is cheap either way. A query embeds one short question in milliseconds on a CPU. So the GPU question is only about bulk indexing of large/changing corpora.

For your hardware specifically:

  • Small models exist for exactly this — a 384-dim model like all-MiniLM is built to run fast on CPU, trading some retrieval quality for "runs anywhere."
  • Apple Silicon isn't a plain CPU: its unified-memory GPU can accelerate embedding via PyTorch's mps backend — no discrete GPU needed. (PyTorch Introducing Accelerated PyTorch Training on Mac, MPS backend in PyTorch 1.12; requires macOS 12.3+; unified memory removes CPU↔GPU copy overhead — Hugging Face Apple-Silicon docs.)
  • Or sidestep owning a GPU entirely: a hosted embedding API does the bulk grinding on their hardware and hands you vectors.
↻ predict (this opens the next topic)
At query time the slow part isn't embedding one short question — it's finding the nearest vectors among 5 million stored ones. Is that search naturally parallel-and-simple like embedding, or does it need something cleverer than brute force?
Brute-force comparison against all 5M vectors per query is too slow to scale — so it needs something cleverer: approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search, which gives up a sliver of exactness for an enormous speedup. That's the reason vector databases exist as a category — and the next thing to learn.
12 · here be dragons

Where intuition breaks

RAG doesn't make the model smarterIts answer quality is capped by its search quality. Wrong pages → a confident, wrong answer grounded in irrelevant text. It reduces ungrounded gaps only when retrieval is good.
the model isn't the documentsA random N-array is worthless; the value is that this array lands near similar-meaning text. The worth lives in the learned weights, not the multiplication. "It's just numbers" is true of the output and misses the point.
embeddings encode topic, not truth"Earth is flat" and "Earth is round" point nearly the same way. No similarity metric knows which is correct.
never mix modelsVectors from two different embedders live in incompatible spaces — comparing them is garbage. One model embeds both documents and queries; switching = full re-index.
cheap-but-bad retrieval is the costliest thingPicking an embedder on price alone, never measuring, then blaming the generator (Opus/GPT) when answers are wrong — when the real culprit was a weak embedder feeding wrong pages.
"embeddings need a GPU"You need throughput proportional to your corpus size and update rate — not the word "embedding." Most real corpora embed in a coffee break, once, on CPU or Apple-Silicon MPS.

15 · faq

Questions you're about to have

Is "adding relevant context to the prompt" the same as RAG?
The generation half is identical. The difference is automation: RAG decides which chunks are relevant to a brand-new question against a library too big to paste, and fetches them at query time. Pasting context yourself is valid when you already know the right doc; RAG earns its keep when you don't, and can't, by hand.
Is Opus 4.8 (or ChatGPT) my embedding model?
No. Those are generators. The embedder is a separate model you choose independently — Voyage, OpenAI's embeddings endpoint, or a local model. Anthropic doesn't ship its own embedder and points to third-party providers. (docs.claude.com Embeddings.)
Higher dimensions are always better, right?
No. More dimensions can capture richer meaning but with diminishing returns, more storage, and slower search. The right N is task-dependent — benchmark on your own data. Matryoshka-trained models even let you truncate dimensions to trade a little accuracy for big savings.
Local or paid embedder?
Start local and free — right for private data, small-to-medium English corpora, prototypes. Measure retrieval on your own queries. Reach for paid only when a factor forces it: weak retrieval on your data, longer inputs than the local model accepts, a specialised domain, multilingual needs, or scale where running it yourself costs more than the API.
Do I need a GPU?
Only for bulk offline indexing of large or fast-changing corpora — and even then, embedding once overnight on CPU is often fine. Query-time embeds one short question in milliseconds. Small models run fast on CPU; Apple Silicon accelerates via PyTorch's MPS backend; or a hosted API does the grinding for you.
What's a vector database for?
It stores embeddings and runs approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search — finding the closest vectors among millions without comparing against all of them. Brute force doesn't scale; ANN trades a sliver of exactness for a big speedup. That's the next concept after this page.
